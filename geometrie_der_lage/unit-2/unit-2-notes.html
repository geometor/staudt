Unit 2 Critical Notes: Desargues, Klein’s Continuity Gap & Darboux¶

Scholarly & Constructive Commentary on Geometrie der Lage (1847), §§ 6–8

  • Why Art. 70 Lifts into Three Dimensions (:person:`Hilbert <hilbert>` Chapter V): Von Staudt’s breathtakingly short uniqueness proof in Art. 70 uses three-dimensional spatial incidence! In 1899 (Grundlagen Kap. V, geometor/hilbert), David Hilbert proved that in a single plane without 3D space axioms, quadrangle uniqueness is equivalent to Desargues’s Theorem: in a non-Desarguesian plane (such as the Moulton plane), different quadrangles on the same collinear triad $(A, B, C)$ yield different fourth points $D$! In geometor.staudt.quadrangle, prove_symbolic_quadrangle_uniqueness() proves over $\mathbb{Q}(c, u, v, t)$ that in any Desarguesian coordinate plane $D_x = \frac{c}{2c - 1}$ is algebraically invariant across all off-line parameters $(u, v, t)$.

  • The Klein–Darboux–Pasch History of Art. 96: In Art. 96, von Staudt concluded that fixing three points $A, B, C$ fixes every point of the line because repeated harmonic fourths generate an everywhere dense Möbius net. Felix Klein (1873) observed two subtleties:

    1. Density of the rational harmonic scale requires an order and continuity (Archimedean/Dedekind) axiom—subsequently supplied by Moritz Pasch (1882).

    2. Over the complex field $\mathbb{C}$, complex conjugation $z \mapsto \bar{z}$ preserves all harmonic cross-ratios $(-1 = \overline{-1})$ and fixes $0, 1, \infty$ without being projective! Over $\mathbb{R}$, however, Jean-Gaston Darboux (1880) proved that every harmonic-preserving bijection of $\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{R})$ automatically preserves positive squares (hence order and continuity) and is therefore strictly projective—completely vindicating von Staudt over the real line! Over an arbitrary field $\mathbb{F}$, a harmonic-preserving bijection is semilinear (projective iff $\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{F}) = \{1\}$).

  • Russell’s 1897 Critique vs. 1903 Vindication: In An Essay on the Foundations of Geometry (1897, §§ 114–120) (geometor/bertrand-russell), Bertrand Russell initially suspected von Staudt’s quadrangle construction of hidden metrical circularity; by The Principles of Mathematics (1903, Ch. XLV, §§ 365–370), armed with Pasch and Veblen’s precursors, Russell retracted his objection and hailed von Staudt’s quadrangle reduction as one of the supreme achievements of nineteenth-century logic of relations.
